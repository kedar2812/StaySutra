Thirty-two kilometres, Nagaon to Kashid. Google will tell you twenty-five
minutes. It is wrong, and being wrong is the best thing about it.

## The road

It runs behind the beaches rather than along them, which means the sea appears
and disappears. You get a kilometre of coconut plantation, a village, a bridge
over a creek, and then the road climbs a low headland and the whole bay opens up
at once. Then it drops back into trees and does it again.

The surface is good — this is a maintained state road — and the traffic is local:
fishing tempos in the morning, school autos at four, almost nothing between.

## When

Late afternoon, moving south. The light comes across the water from your right
and everything goes gold for about forty minutes. That window is the reason
people who have done this ride once come back to do it again.

Sunrise works too, but the light is behind the trees and the beaches are still
grey.

## The thing nobody mentions

Sand. It drifts across the tarmac wherever the road runs close to a beach access
point, and it collects in exactly the place you would want to put your front
wheel on the way into a corner. It is not dangerous if you expect it. It is very
dangerous if you do not.

Brake early, stay upright through the drifts, and look for the tell — a lighter
patch across the road surface.

## Where to stop

**Korlai.** A Portuguese fort on a headland about halfway down, reached by a
short track off the main road. Mostly ruined, entirely empty, and the view back
north along the coast is the best on the route.

**Revdanda.** The creek bridge. Pull over on the far side and look back — the old
fort wall runs into the water.

**Kashid itself.** Long, wide, and the water is genuinely clean. The parking is
sand, so stop before you commit.

## Getting there

If you are coming from Mumbai, take the RoRo ferry from Bhaucha Dhakka with the
bike on board. It saves you the entire creek detour through Panvel and Pen, and
it puts you at Mandwa with the whole coast ahead of you. Check the sailing times
the night before — they change with the tide and they change in the monsoon.
